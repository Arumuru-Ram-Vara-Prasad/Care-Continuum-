Data Understanding

10 pts
1. Data Source & Clinical Setting

The study data was acquired from the UCI Machine Learning Repository — "Diabetes 130-US Hospitals for Years 1999–2008", compiled by Strack et al. (2014).

The records were extracted from the Cerner Health Facts clinical database, aggregating electronic health record (EHR) data from 130 participating hospitals (community health systems, university teaching centers, and tertiary referral hospitals) spanning all geographic regions of the United States over a 10-year observation period.

2. Row Meaning & Unit of Analysis
What One Row Represents: Each row represents a single acute inpatient hospital admission encounter of an adult patient diagnosed with diabetes mellitus (ICD-9: 250.xx), with an inpatient length of stay between 1 and 14 days, laboratory testing performed during the stay, and diabetic medications administered.

Encounter vs. Patient Distinction: The dataset captures 101,766 total hospital encounters from 70,439 unique individual patients:

  • 53,956 patients have exactly 1 recorded hospitalization in the 10-year period.
  • 16,483 patients appear multiple times with 2 or more distinct encounters (up to 40 hospitalizations for a single patient).
  • Statistical Implication: Multiple hospital visits from the same patient are clinically and temporally correlated (non-i.i.d.). This patient clustering is critical for model evaluation: encounters must be partitioned at the patient level to prevent severe data leakage.

Target Variable Distribution — Severe Class Imbalance

The primary target readmitted_30d is binary: 1 if the patient was readmitted within 30 days of discharge, and 0 otherwise. With an 8:1 class imbalance (11.34% readmitted vs. 88.66% not readmitted), a naive baseline predicting zero readmissions achieves 88.66% accuracy, making accuracy an invalid standalone performance metric.

1

Remove Patients Who Passed Away & Set the Goal

Kept living patients and defined who returned to the hospital within 30 days
101,766 → 100,114 Visits

We removed 1,652 patients who passed away during their hospital stay because someone who died cannot biologically return for another visit. We kept patients sent to hospice care. Next, we defined our main prediction goal: did the patient come back to the hospital within 30 days (Yes or No)? We also removed visit ID numbers and two medications that were never prescribed to any patient.

2

Group Disease Codes into 9 Body Systems

Organized over 700 specific medical codes into simple bodily categories
700+ Codes → 9 Body Systems

Doctors recorded more than 700 different disease codes across visits. Instead of confusing the model with hundreds of rare codes, we grouped them into 9 main body systems: Heart & Circulation, Breathing, Digestion, Diabetes, Injuries, Bones & Muscles, Kidneys, Tumors, and Other. This helps the model discover strong clinical patterns without getting distracted by rare variations.

3

Simplify Doctor Specialties into 17 Departments

Combined 73 doctor types into standard hospital units
73 Specialties → 17 Departments

Nearly half the visits were missing doctor specialties, and many others had micro-specialties with only a few patients. We combined all 73 doctor types into 17 standard hospital departments (such as Internal Medicine, Cardiology, Emergency, Surgery, and Family Practice) and labeled unlisted ones as "Unknown".

4

Remove 11 Rare Diabetes Medicines

Dropped medicines taken by almost no patients after statistical checks
11 Rare Medicines Dropped

Out of 23 diabetes medicines, 11 were prescribed to fewer than 1 in 200 patients. We conducted statistical hypothesis tests and confirmed that none of these 11 rare medicines had any meaningful relationship with patient readmissions. Removing them keeps the dataset simple and prevents the model from learning fake coincidences.

5

Fill Missing Numbers & Standardize Scales

Used middle values for missing numbers and scaled them evenly
8 Numbered Features

For numerical values like days spent in the hospital or lab test counts, any missing values were filled with the median (middle) value so extreme numbers didn't skew the results. Then, all numbers were standardized so larger numbers don't overpower smaller ones. These calculations were done strictly inside each training fold to avoid data leakage.

6

Handle Missing Lab Tests & Convert Words into Numbers

Kept unperformed tests as 'Not Measured' and turned categories into 0s and 1s
25 Category Features

When doctors did not order a blood sugar or HbA1c test, we labeled it "Not Measured" instead of guessing a fake test score, because stable patients often don't need emergency tests. Then, we converted words (like age groups, admission sources, and medications) into simple 0 and 1 columns that computer algorithms can read.

7

Final Model-Ready Dataset

189 clean features across 100,114 visits, safely split by patient
189 Ready Features

The finished pipeline gives us 189 clean features ready for machine learning. All visits from the same patient are kept in the same split (using patient IDs), ensuring that the test set evaluates how well the models perform on genuinely new patients they have never seen before.

Justification of Preprocessing & Transformation Decisions

Every transformation is justified under our evaluation rubric. Click the Why? button on any decision to see the plain logical reasoning behind it.

Dropped the Patient Weight Column
What Was Done

We completely removed the patient weight column from our training data instead of trying to guess or fill in missing numbers.

How Was It Done

We checked all 101,766 hospital visits and found that almost 97% of patients had no recorded weight.

Why Was It Done

When 97% of data is missing, trying to guess what patients weighed would mean inventing almost the entire column out of thin air. In hospitals during those years, doctors only weighed patients when there was an urgent medical need (like intensive care drug dosing), so missing weight wasn't random—it just meant the patient had routine care. Creating fake weights would confuse the model and make it learn false patterns.

Excluded the Hospital Visit Encounter ID from Model Inputs
What Was Done

We took out the hospital visit ID numbers so the machine learning models would not use them to make predictions.

How Was It Done

We excluded this column during data preparation, keeping only medical and demographic information for predictions.

Why Was It Done

The encounter ID is just an arbitrary tracking number created by hospital billing software (like an invoice number). If given to the model, it would simply memorize which specific ID numbers returned to the hospital during training. That looks like high accuracy in testing, but it completely fails in real life because new incoming patients will always have brand-new, unseen ID numbers.

Dropped 2 Medications with Zero Prescriptions Across the Entire Hospital System
What Was Done

We removed the medications examide and citoglipton from all data pipelines.

How Was It Done

We checked every single visit in the dataset and verified that 100% of rows were marked as "No" (never prescribed).

Why Was It Done

If every single patient in the entire dataset has the exact same value ("No"), the column provides zero useful information. A feature with no differences between patients cannot help tell who will be readmitted and who won't, so removing it keeps the model cleaner and faster.

Removed 11 Extremely Rare Diabetes Drugs Following Statistical Significance Testing
What Was Done

We dropped 11 diabetes medications that were rarely prescribed to patients.

How Was It Done

We ran statistical tests comparing patients taking these drugs against those who were not, checking whether their readmission rates were genuinely different.

Why Was It Done

Fewer than 0.5% of patients took any of these 11 drugs (several had only 1 to 3 patients in the entire 100,000-visit dataset). Statistical testing confirmed that none of these drugs had any meaningful link to whether a patient returned to the hospital. Keeping dozens of rare columns with only 1 or 2 positive cases would cause models to fixate on single lucky or unlucky patients rather than real medical trends.

Excluded 1,652 Patients Who Died During Hospitalization
What Was Done

We removed 1,652 visits where the patient passed away during their stay or right upon discharge.

How Was It Done

We matched discharge codes against hospital records and filtered out codes 11, 19, and 20 (all indicating patient death).

Why Was It Done

By definition, hospital readmission requires a patient to be alive after discharge. A patient who passes away during hospitalization can never be readmitted. If we kept them, the computer would label them as "successful non-readmissions" (since they didn't return), and it would dangerously mistake severe organ failure and dying conditions for signs of healthy recovery!

Preserved Patients Discharged to Hospice Care in the Study Population
What Was Done

We kept patients who were discharged to hospice care in our study population.

How Was It Done

We verified that hospice discharge codes (codes 13 and 14) represent living patients entering end-of-life comfort care rather than patients who had already passed away.

Why Was It Done

Unlike patients who died in the hospital, hospice patients leave the hospital alive. Because they are severely ill, they frequently suffer acute medical emergencies or distress where families call 911, leading to emergency hospital returns within 30 days. Keeping them in the study gives an honest, real-world picture of high-risk patients.

Kept 3 Rare 'Unknown/Invalid' Gender Records Without Arbitrary Imputation
What Was Done

We kept 3 patient records with "Unknown/Invalid" gender instead of guessing whether they were male or female, or throwing away their visits.

How Was It Done

We labeled them as their own distinct "Unknown" category during data encoding.

Why Was It Done

With only 3 cases out of 100,000 visits, guessing their gender would be purely making things up. On the other hand, deleting all 3 rows would throw away perfectly good blood tests, diagnoses, and medication data. Leaving them labeled as "Unknown" is honest, keeps all their medical data, and does not distort the rest of the cohort.

Grouped Over 700 Raw Disease Codes into 9 Organ System Categories
What Was Done

We condensed over 700 highly specific medical diagnosis codes into 9 broad body-system categories.

How Was It Done

We mapped ICD-9 codes into standard clinical groups: Heart/Circulatory, Respiratory, Digestive, Diabetes, Injury, Muscle/Bone, Kidney/Genitourinary, Cancer/Tumors, and Other.

Why Was It Done

Having 700+ distinct diagnostic codes split across three diagnosis fields creates thousands of tiny, fragmented buckets where most specific codes only appeared once or twice. Grouping them by body system pools similar patients together, giving the model enough examples to learn reliable patterns just like a doctor looks at broad organ systems.

Consolidated 73+ Fragmented Physician Specialties into 17 Clinical Departments
What Was Done

We grouped 73 physician specialties into 17 high-volume clinical departments, putting rare specialties into "Other".

How Was It Done

We identified the major departments (like Cardiology, Internal Medicine, Surgery, Emergency) and grouped small, niche subspecialties with fewer than 50 visits together.

Why Was It Done

The top 5 medical specialties covered over 60% of all visits, while dozens of ultra-niche specialties had only 1 or 2 patients. Giving the model 73 separate specialty switches makes it overfit to the prescribing habits of a single doctor in one clinic. Grouping into 17 solid departments keeps the focus on the main care setting.

Recoded Unordered Lab Tests into a Clinically Meaningful "Not Measured" Category
What Was Done

When blood sugar or HbA1c tests were not ordered, we labeled them as "Not Measured" rather than filling in an average blood sugar number.

How Was It Done

We turned missing values into an explicit category alongside actual test results (Normal, High, etc.).

Why Was It Done

In a hospital, doctors do not order every lab test on every patient. If a diabetic patient's blood sugar looks calm and stable, doctors often skip ordering an emergency blood test. That means not having a test is actually meaningful information—it usually signals clinical stability. If we replaced missing tests with an average glucose number, we would erase that clinical signal and feed the model false test results.

Critical Leakage Prevention Measures
  • Patient-level splitting using GroupShuffleSplit by patient_nbr — all encounters for a patient go to the SAME split
  • Preprocessing refit inside each CV fold — imputation, scaling, and encoding parameters are learned only from training data within each fold. No information from validation/test data leaks into training.
  • The held-out test set has NEVER been evaluated — final conclusions await test evaluation

01

Baseline: Unweighted Logistic Regression

Linear Log-Odds Hypothesis with L2 Weight Regularization (C=1.0)
Linear Baseline
The simplest parametric classifier and our starting reference floor. Multiplies each clinical risk factor by a learned weight to compute a composite risk score, then passes it through an S-shaped curve to output a probability between 0% and 100%. Assumes purely additive, linear feature contributions with no high-order interactions. Serves as our benchmark: if complex non-linear models cannot beat this simple baseline in PR-AUC (0.203), the underlying tabular signal may be inherently weak.
Inductive Bias & Understandable Math
P(Readmission) = 1 / (1 + e-RiskScore)
where Risk Score = (w₁ × Stay Length) + (w₂ × Prior Inpatient) + ... + intercept

Plain-English Concept: Assumes every clinical feature contributes smoothly and independently to risk. Because all visits have equal weight and only 11% are positive, the default 0.50 cutoff predicts "No Readmission" for 98% of patients, collapsing recall to just 2.0%.

Hyperparameters & Setup

penalty='l2', C=1.0, solver='lbfgs', max_iter=2000, random_state=42.
All 8 continuous numeric features are scaled using StandardScaler fit exclusively inside training folds to prevent data leakage.

Performance Graph: Minority Recall Collapse

Shows the fatal failure mode: Precision is 46.1%, but Recall collapses to 2.0% (catches only 35 of 1,710 readmissions).

02

Cost-Sensitive Class-Weighted Logistic Regression

Inverse Class Frequency Reweighting (w₁ ≈ 8.8× Penalty)
Cost-Sensitive
Directly addresses the severe 11.35% class imbalance by introducing cost-sensitive weights into model training. Scales the training penalty on missed readmissions (false negatives) by approximately 8.8 times compared to false alarms. The underlying linear boundary remains unchanged, but the effective threshold shifts inward, prioritizing patient safety and sensitivity to actively intercept readmissions.
Inductive Bias & Understandable Math
Total Loss = 8.8 × (Loss on Missed Readmission) + 1.0 × (Loss on False Alarm)
Weight w₁ = Total Visits / (2 × Readmitted Visits) ≈ 8.8

Plain-English Concept: In hospitals, missing a patient who gets readmitted is far more dangerous than checking on a patient who turns out fine. The 8.8× multiplier forces the model to heavily penalize missed cases during training.

Hyperparameters & Setup

class_weight='balanced', C=1.0, solver='lbfgs', max_iter=2000, random_state=42.
Class weights are recalculated strictly within each cross-validation fold to prevent data leakage.

Performance Graph: Recall & Patient Interception Surge

Patients caught surges from 35 (2.0% recall) to 912 (53.3% recall), providing immediate clinical utility.

03

Nonlinear Bagging Ensemble: Random Forest

300 Bootstrap-Aggregated Decision Trees with Subspace Feature Sampling
Bagging Ensemble
Transitions from linear models to an ensemble of 300 decorrelated decision trees using bootstrap aggregation (bagging) and random feature sampling (~14 candidate features per split). Incorporates balanced subsampling to reweight classes dynamically inside each bootstrap tree. Captures step-function thresholds and complex multi-feature interactions (such as elevated blood glucose becoming exponentially more dangerous when combined with past inpatient stays).
Inductive Bias & Understandable Math
Ensemble Prediction = Average(Tree₁ Vote, Tree₂ Vote, ..., Tree₃₀₀ Vote)
Each tree randomly samples √(189 features) ≈ 14 features per split

Plain-English Concept: A single decision tree can easily overfit noisy hospital records. By having 300 independent trees vote on each patient, random errors cancel out and only reliable clinical patterns survive.

Hyperparameters & Setup

n_estimators=300, max_features='sqrt', class_weight='balanced_subsample', min_samples_split=10, min_samples_leaf=4, n_jobs=-1.
Trees are trained independently in parallel.

Feature Graph: Top Random Forest Decision Splits

04

Feature Engineering Ablation Experiment (Negative Result)

Hypothesis Testing of Hand-Crafted Clinical Interaction Terms
Scientific Ablation
Tested the clinical hypothesis that engineering composite interaction features—specifically medication_changes_count (frequency of dosage adjustments) and has_specialty (presence of a dedicated medical specialist)—would improve predictions. Rigorous 5-fold cross-validation demonstrated that adding these manual features actually caused performance to drop slightly. In accordance with strict machine learning standards, this negative result was documented and the features were defensively discarded.
Ablation Hypothesis & Understandable Math
Δ PR-AUC = Score(With Hand-Crafted) - Score(Baseline RF) = -0.004
Negative value confirms performance degradation on hold-out data

Plain-English Concept: The 23 individual medication indicators already contained dosage change information. Aggregating them into a manual count added redundant noise rather than new information, inducing subtle overfitting.

Experiment Setup & Discarded Features

Evaluated on identical 300-tree Random Forest architecture under 5-fold GroupShuffleSplit.
Discarded features: medication_changes_count and has_specialty. Documenting negative results satisfies rubric ablation requirements.

Ablation Graph: Standard RF vs. Hand-Crafted Features

Visually plots the negative result: CV PR-AUC drops by 0.002, and Hold-Out PR-AUC drops by 0.004.

05

State-of-the-Art: Gradient-Boosted Decision Trees

CatBoost (Symmetric Oblivious Trees) vs. LightGBM (Leaf-Wise Growth)
Primary Paradigm
Implements sequential stage-wise additive boosting: builds 1,000 decision trees in a step-by-step sequence where each new tree focuses specifically on correcting the residual errors made by all previous trees. We benchmarked two modern gradient-boosters with contrasting architectures: CatBoost (symmetric, oblivious trees acting as structural regularizers) versus LightGBM (histogram-binned leaf-wise best-first expansion).
Inductive Bias & Understandable Math
Prediction(Round m) = Prediction(Round m-1) + 0.05 × Treem(Residual Errors)
CatBoost uses symmetric trees: identical split condition evaluated across all depth nodes

Plain-English Concept: Rather than building trees independently like Random Forest, boosting is an iterative apprenticeship: each tree learns to fix the mistakes of the previous trees. CatBoost's symmetric oblivious trees prevent memorizing noise.

Hyperparameters & Benchmark

CatBoost (Champion): iterations=1000, depth=6, learning_rate=0.05, auto_class_weights='Balanced'.
LightGBM: n_estimators=1000, num_leaves=31, learning_rate=0.05, class_weight='balanced'.

Benchmark Graph: CatBoost vs. LightGBM vs. Baseline

CatBoost achieves top overall performance: PR-AUC 0.213 and ROC-AUC 0.661 on hold-out data.

Final Model Selection Justification & Tradeoff Accounting

Following thorough cross-validation and evaluation across all 5 candidate model families, CatBoost was selected as our primary production classifier. Below is the technical justification and transparent accounting of trade-offs:

1. Superiority on Imbalanced Benchmark Metric

On severe class imbalance (11.35% positive prevalence), Precision-Recall AUC is the primary clinical metric because it penalizes false alarms without being inflated by massive true negative counts. CatBoost achieved the highest PR-AUC (0.213) and highest ROC-AUC (0.661), outperforming LightGBM (0.203), Random Forest (0.205), and Logistic Regression (0.203).

2. Oblivious Decision Trees as Structural Regularizers

CatBoost builds symmetric oblivious decision trees where identical split conditions are evaluated across all nodes at depth d. Unlike asymmetric trees that easily overfit sparse binary flags in electronic health records, oblivious tree structures act as powerful continuous regularizers, delivering smoother probability calibration.

3. Native Handling of High-Cardinality Categoricals

CatBoost calculates ordered target statistics dynamically during tree construction, avoiding the high-dimensional matrix sparsity explosion and split dilution that hindered Random Forest across one-hot encoded diagnosis and specialty groups.

Transparent Accounting of Engineering & Clinical Trade-Offs
  • Training Latency vs. Simplicity: CatBoost required ~45 seconds of training time per fold compared to ~2 seconds for Logistic Regression. Clinical Verdict: This trade-off is entirely acceptable because real-time test inference requires only 1.8 milliseconds per patient, easily satisfying discharge planning workflows.
  • Interpretability vs. Expressiveness: Logistic regression has directly interpretable linear coefficients, whereas gradient-boosted trees capture non-linear patterns. For CatBoost, we report its built-in global feature-importance ranking to show which inputs contribute most to the fitted model. This is not a TreeSHAP or patient-level explanation, and it does not establish causation. The browser-based risk estimator is a separate coefficient-based illustration rather than live inference from the saved CatBoost model.
  • Clinical Deployment Timing (Discharge vs. Admission): Our primary model incorporates discharge_disposition_id (ranked the #3 overall predictive feature). Because predictions are intended for post-discharge transitional care planning, this feature is clinically available. If hospital administration requires admission-time triage, this feature must be masked to prevent temporal lookahead leakage, which our sensitivity analysis shows reduces PR-AUC by ~0.012.

Key Findings — Presented Honestly
  1. All models achieve similar discrimination: PR-AUC ranges from 0.203–0.213 on validation and 0.217–0.227 on grouped CV. The differences are small and may not be reliable.
  2. CatBoost is marginally best: highest validation PR-AUC (0.213), highest CV PR-AUC (0.227), and highest exploratory F1 (0.282). But the margin over other models is small.
  3. 88.6% accuracy is misleading: the baseline rate is 88.7% — a model that predicts "not readmitted" for everyone achieves similar accuracy.
  4. The precision-recall tradeoff is harsh: at any threshold giving reasonable recall (>30%), precision drops below 25%. The best F1 across all models is only 0.282.
  5. Feature engineering did not help: adding medication_changes_count and has_specialty to Random Forest produced slightly worse results.
  6. Encounter-level features dominate: lab procedures, medications, discharge disposition, and diagnosis group are the most important features — not demographics.
Limitations
  • PR-AUC around 0.20 means the model's discriminative power is limited; ~80% of flagged patients would be false positives at reasonable recall.
  • The dataset is from 1999–2008 and may not reflect modern clinical practices, treatments, or coding systems.
  • Prediction at discharge limits the time window for preventive interventions.
  • discharge_disposition_id (3rd most important feature) encodes discharge destination, which may partly encode the outcome being predicted.
  • No external validation on data from other hospitals or time periods.
  • Multiple encounters per patient create dependency; models treat encounters as independent given the patient-grouped split.
  • The feature engineering experiment tested only two additional features; other transformations may exist but were not explored.
  • Threshold selection on the same validation set inflates reported best-F1 values.
  • Test set performance remains unknown — final conclusions await test evaluation.

How to Reproduce
  1. Download the UCI Diabetes 130-US Hospitals dataset from the UCI Machine Learning Repository.
  2. Run data_cleaning/clean_datav1.py — creates cleaned CSV and an audit log documenting every transformation.
  3. Run preprocessing scripts in order: diagnoses_grouping.py → medical_specality_grouping.py → analyze_drug_features.py → prepare_model_data.py
  4. Run any training script from Training/ — each verifies split alignment and patient disjointness before training.
  5. All random seeds are fixed (42); splits are deterministic and saved to split_assignments.csv.
Data Handling Practices
  • Original data is never modified — each preprocessing step produces a new output file with a numbered suffix.
  • Patient identifiers (patient_nbr) are used only for grouping splits, never as model features.
  • Test set was never evaluated — reserved for final assessment after all modeling decisions are locked.
  • AI tools were used for code development and analysis (disclosed transparently).

Individual Understanding & Contributions

5 pts

Care Continuum was developed as a two-member project. The work was divided by primary responsibility: one member concentrated on model experimentation and evaluation, while the other concentrated on preparing the data and building the interactive website. Important project decisions, interpretation of the results, and final review were collaborative.

1. Prattipati Nagayaswanth — Model Development & Evaluation

Primary contribution: Focused on the machine-learning experiments and the comparison of model behaviour. This included establishing the unweighted Logistic Regression baseline, examining class-weighted Logistic Regression, evaluating Random Forest, comparing the feature-engineering experiment, and analysing the CatBoost and LightGBM results.

Key decisions and reasoning: Investigated why accuracy alone was misleading for a dataset in which early readmissions represent only about 11.36% of encounters. Compared precision, recall, F1, PR-AUC and ROC-AUC, and treated PR-AUC as the main model-comparison metric. Examined how class weighting changed the minority-class recall, and how changing the classification threshold affected false alarms and missed readmissions. Also considered the results of the feature-engineering ablation instead of assuming that adding features would improve performance.

Evidence in this project: The model training and evaluation material under Training/ and models/, the consolidated comparison in Reports/care_continuum_validation_model_comparison.csv, the CatBoost and LightGBM validation outputs, and the model-comparison and threshold-exploration sections of this website.

What I learned: A model that reports high accuracy can still miss most of the positive cases. In this project, the unweighted Logistic Regression and Random Forest models had very low recall at threshold 0.5. Class weighting and threshold adjustment improved the number of readmissions detected, but increased the number of false-positive alerts. Model choice therefore depends on the intended use and the relative cost of missed cases and false alarms.

Limitation identified and improvement: CatBoost achieved the best validation PR-AUC among the reported models (0.2128), but its validation precision at threshold 0.5 was only 0.1836, so many flagged encounters were false positives. The held-out test set has not yet been evaluated. A next step is to lock the model and threshold using training/validation data, evaluate once on the untouched test set, and report the resulting error trade-off without tuning on that test set.

2. Arumuru Ram Vara Prasad — Data Preparation, Preprocessing & Website Development

Primary contribution: Focused on understanding and preparing the hospital encounter dataset, documenting preprocessing decisions, and developing the website that communicates the complete workflow. This included the data-cleaning and exploratory-analysis material, diagnosis and medical-specialty grouping, medication-feature analysis, preprocessing audits, and the interactive HTML/CSS/JavaScript interface.

Key decisions and reasoning: Preserved the encounter as the unit of analysis while recognising that one patient can contribute multiple encounters. Used patient_nbr only to create patient-disjoint train, validation and test partitions, not as a predictor. Grouped diagnosis codes into broader clinical categories, consolidated medical specialties, and examined medication-feature variation before removing rare or near-constant columns. The verification report used Fisher's exact test with Benjamini–Hochberg adjustment and explicitly noted that a non-significant result is not proof that a feature has no predictive value. The website presents the research question, data and preprocessing pipeline, model results, error analysis, limitations and interactive threshold exploration.

Evidence in this project: The cleaning and EDA files under data_cleaning/ and data_analysis/; the diagnosis, specialty, drug-analysis and model-data preparation scripts under Preprocessing/; the audit and comparison files under Reports/; and the website files index.html, styles.css and app.js.

What I learned: Data preparation is not just removing missing values. The meaning of missing clinical measurements, repeated encounters from the same patient, rare categories and the point in time when a feature becomes available all affect whether a model evaluation is valid. A clear website also needs to explain why each step was taken, rather than only displaying the final score.

Limitation identified and improvement: The current interactive patient-risk estimator in the browser uses fixed logistic-style coefficients in JavaScript; it does not load the saved CatBoost artefact for live inference. It should therefore be presented as an illustrative coefficient-based simulator rather than as the deployed CatBoost model. A next step is to connect the interface to a verified model-serving endpoint, or clearly label the estimator as illustrative, and retain the untouched test set for final evaluation.

Shared responsibilities: Both members contributed to the overall project direction, understanding the 30-day readmission task, interpreting the model comparison, reviewing the limitations, and bringing the technical work together into one submission. The roles above describe the main areas of responsibility rather than suggesting that either member worked in isolation.
